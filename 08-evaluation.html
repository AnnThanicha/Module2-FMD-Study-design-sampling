8. Evaluation: OR, RR, chi-square and interpretation

In this lecture we focus on the statistical tools used to analyse categorical data. The data usually come from a cross-tabulation or a 2×2 table. The tools use the same table, but they measure different things:

ToolWhat it measuresQuestion it answersTypical design
Chi-square testWhether an association exists (p-value)Is the pattern in the table more than chance?Any 2×2 table
Risk ratio (RR)Size of the association, as a ratio of risksHow many times higher is the risk in the exposed?Cohort, trial, cross-sectional
Odds ratio (OR)Size of the association, as a ratio of oddsHow many times higher are the odds in the exposed?Case-control, any design
Mantel–Haenszel ORSize of the association after adjusting for a confounderWhat is the OR once the stratifying variable is accounted for?Stratified 2×2 tables

The chi-square test tells you whether there is an association. RR and OR tell you how strong it is. The Mantel–Haenszel OR tells you how strong it is after controlling for a confounder.

8.1 Chi-square test

The chi-square test tests a hypothesis about the whole table.
H₀ (null hypothesis): there is no association between the exposure and the disease; the two variables are independent.
Hₐ (alternative hypothesis): there is an association between the exposure and the disease.
It compares the observed counts with the counts expected if H₀ were true. The further the observed table is from the expected one, the less likely H₀ is.

Example: cross-sectional survey of lameness and flooring. A survey of 200 dairy cows records the type of flooring and whether each cow was lame.

LameNot lameTotal
Concrete floor24 (a)76 (b)100
Rubber mat floor10 (c)90 (d)100
Total34166200

Step 1 – hypotheses. H₀: lameness is not associated with flooring. Hₐ: lameness is associated with flooring.

Step 2 – expected counts under H₀. Expected = (row total × column total) / N.

E(a) = 100 × 34 / 200 = 17    E(b) = 100 × 166 / 200 = 83
E(c) = 100 × 34 / 200 = 17    E(d) = 100 × 166 / 200 = 83

Step 3 – compute the statistic. Add (O – E)² / E for each of the four cells.

CellObserved (O)Expected (E)(O – E)² / E
a: concrete, lame2417(24 – 17)² / 17 = 2.88
b: concrete, not lame7683(76 – 83)² / 83 = 0.59
c: rubber, lame1017(10 – 17)² / 17 = 2.88
d: rubber, not lame9083(90 – 83)² / 83 = 0.59
χ² = 2.88 + 0.59 + 2.88 + 0.59 = 6.95

Step 4 – p-value. Degrees of freedom = (rows – 1) × (columns – 1) = 1. The critical value at α = 0.05 is 3.84 (see the chi-square table). Here χ² = 6.95 (p ≈ 0.008, Pearson, no continuity correction). With Yates' continuity correction, χ² = 5.99 (p ≈ 0.014). State which version you use.

Interpretation: there is evidence of an association between flooring and lameness (p < 0.05). The p-value does not measure the size of the association, and it does not prove causation. Use RR or OR (8.2, 8.3) for the size.

CautionOrdinary chi-square assumes independent observations.
Use Fisher's exact test when any expected count is < 5.

8.2 Risk ratio (cohort study)

Use the risk ratio for cohort studies and trials, where you follow animals over time and can measure risk.

Example: cohort study of colostrum and calf diarrhoea. 200 newborn calves were followed for 30 days. Colostrum intake was recorded at birth.

DiarrhoeaNo diarrhoeaTotal
Inadequate colostrum (exposed)30 (a)70 (b)100
Adequate colostrum (unexposed)10 (c)90 (d)100
Total40160200

Step 1 – risk in each group.

Risk in exposed = a / (a + b) = 30 / 100 = 0.30 (30%)
Risk in unexposed = c / (c + d) = 10 / 100 = 0.10 (10%)

Step 2 – risk ratio.

RR = 0.30 / 0.10 = 3.0

Step 3 – 95% confidence interval.

SE of ln RR = sqrt(1/30 – 1/100 + 1/10 – 1/100) = sqrt(0.1133) = 0.337
ln RR = ln 3.0 = 1.099
95% CI = exp(1.099 ± 1.96 × 0.337) = exp(0.439) to exp(1.759) = 1.55 to 5.80

Interpretation: “Calves with inadequate colostrum had three times the 30-day risk of diarrhoea compared with calves receiving adequate colostrum (RR 3.0; 95% CI 1.6–5.8).”

Risk difference: 30% – 10% = 20 percentage points (95% CI 9–31 pp). This is the absolute difference. In practical terms, about 20 extra cases per 100 calves are linked to inadequate colostrum. Farmers and managers often find this easier to act on than the 3× relative difference.

RR = 1 means no association. RR > 1 means higher risk in the exposed. RR < 1 means lower risk (a protective factor).

8.3 Odds ratio (unadjusted)

The odds ratio in this section is the unadjusted (crude) odds ratio. It uses all the data in one 2×2 table and does not account for other factors. For an adjusted odds ratio, see 8.4. Use the odds ratio for case-control studies, where risk cannot be calculated.

Example: case-control study of a prior exposure. The study includes 100 cases and 100 controls. Investigators asked about the prior exposure.

CasesControls
Exposed40 (a)25 (b)
Unexposed60 (c)75 (d)
Total100100

Step 1 – odds in each group.

Odds of exposure in cases = a / c = 40 / 60 = 0.667
Odds of exposure in controls = b / d = 25 / 75 = 0.333

Step 2 – odds ratio.

OR = (a × d) / (b × c) = (40 × 75) / (25 × 60) = 3000 / 1500 = 2.0

Step 3 – 95% confidence interval (Woolf).

SE of ln OR = sqrt(1/40 + 1/25 + 1/60 + 1/75) = sqrt(0.095) = 0.308
ln OR = ln 2.0 = 0.693
95% CI = exp(0.693 ± 1.96 × 0.308) = exp(0.089) to exp(1.297) = 1.09 to 3.66

Interpretation: “The odds of prior exposure among cases were twice those among controls (OR 2.0; 95% CI 1.09–3.66).” The interval does not include 1, so the association is statistically significant. Because investigators chose how many cases and controls to include, disease risk cannot be calculated. The OR is the usable measure. Conveniently, the exposure OR equals the disease OR, so it still describes the association with disease.

Why not call the OR a risk ratio?Use the calf cohort table in 8.2: OR = (30 × 90) / (70 × 10) = 2700 / 700 = 3.86 (95% CI 1.77–8.42), but RR = 3.0. The OR is further from 1 than the RR whenever the outcome is common. When the outcome is rare (< ~10%), OR ≈ RR. Do not say “3.86 times the risk”; say “3.86 times the odds”.

8.4 Mantel–Haenszel adjusted odds ratio

A crude (unstratified) odds ratio can be misleading if a third variable (confounder, Section 2.2) affects both the exposure and the outcome.

  • Stratification breaks the data into separate 2 × 2 tables for each level of the confounding variable (for example, separate tables for different age groups).
  • The Mantel–Haenszel procedure combines these stratum-specific odds ratios into one overall adjusted estimate.

It adjusts for one confounder at a time. The steps are:

  1. Split the data into strata by the confounder (for example, trained staff: no / yes).
  2. Make one 2×2 table for each stratum and calculate the stratum-specific OR.
  3. Combine the strata into one MH adjusted OR. Use an online calculator (see the link at the end of this section).
  4. Compare the crude OR with the MH OR.
ORMH = Σ ( aᵢ dᵢ / nᵢ ) / Σ ( bᵢ cᵢ / nᵢ )

a, b, c, d = cells of the 2×2 table in stratum i; nᵢ = total in stratum i

Key assumptions and rules

Homogeneity. Pooling only makes sense if the odds ratio is relatively constant across all strata. If the effect differs widely, you likely have effect modification (interaction) rather than simple confounding. You can test this with Woolf's test or the Breslow–Day test.

Tests of homogeneity. Woolf's test and the Breslow–Day test check whether the odds ratio linking an exposure to an outcome is the same (homogeneous) across the strata or subgroups of a population. Use one of them before pooling with Mantel–Haenszel.
H₀: the odds ratios in all strata are equal.
Hₐ: at least one stratum has a different odds ratio, which suggests effect modification (interaction).
Reading the p-value: a small p-value (for example < 0.05) means the odds ratios differ between strata, so do not pool. A large p-value (≥ 0.05) means there is no evidence that they differ, so pooling is reasonable.

Weighting. The MH estimate is a weighted average. It gives more influence to strata with larger sample sizes and greater precision.

When to use. It is ideal for a single categorical confounder. For multiple or continuous confounders, multivariable logistic regression is preferred.

Example: housing, pneumonia and age. Hypothetical data from 400 calves. Group pens are compared with individual hutches, and the outcome is pneumonia. Age is the possible confounder, so make one 2×2 table for each age group. Young calves are more often kept in group pens and are more susceptible to pneumonia. The two stratum ORs are not equal (2.00 and 3.08), but both are well below the crude OR of 4.55.

Steps 1–2 – numerator Σ(a×d / n) and denominator Σ(b×c / n).

Numerator = (64×30)/200 + (8×148)/200 = 9.60 + 5.92 = 15.52
Denominator = (96×10)/200 + (32×12)/200 = 4.80 + 1.92 = 6.72

Step 3 – Mantel–Haenszel OR.

ORMH (pooled OR) = 15.52 / 6.72 = 2.31 (95% CI 1.24 – 4.30)

Step 4 – compare with the crude OR and check homogeneity.

Change = (4.55 – 2.31) / 2.31 = 0.97, about 97%
Woolf test of homogeneity: χ² = 0.46, 1 df, p = 0.50
Breslow–Day test of homogeneity: χ² = 0.47, 1 df, p = 0.49

Interpretation: the crude OR (4.55) overstates the effect of group pens because young calves are both more often in pens and more susceptible. After adjusting for age, the OR falls to 2.31, a change of about 97% (far above 10%), so age is a confounder. The stratum ORs are not identical, but the homogeneity tests show no evidence that they differ (Woolf p = 0.50; Breslow–Day p = 0.49), so pooling is reasonable. If a homogeneity test gives p < 0.05, the homogeneity assumption is violated and we report the stratified OR for each stratum instead.

How to read the resultCrude and MH ORs differ by more than about 10%: the factor is a confounder. Report the MH OR.
Crude and MH ORs are similar: little confounding by this factor.
Stratum ORs are very different (or a homogeneity test, Woolf or Breslow–Day, has a small p-value): the effect depends on the stratum. Report each stratum OR separately instead of pooling.
