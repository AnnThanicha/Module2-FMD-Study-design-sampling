Risk factors for foot and mouth disease outbreaks in dairy farms

Task 1. Objective of the study

Question: Write the objective of this study, including the population, outcome, place, time period and comparison (Section 1.1).

Model objective

“To quantify the farm-level risk factors associated with FMD outbreaks in dairy farms registered in Mae On, San Kamphaeng and San Sai districts, Chiang Mai Province, northern Thailand, in 2016, by comparing outbreak farms with non-outbreak farms, using odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.”

ElementIn the paper
PopulationDairy farms registered with dairy cooperatives and/or the Chiang Mai Provincial Livestock Office in three districts
OutcomeFMD outbreak at farm level: at least one animal with typical clinical signs (vesicles on feet, mammary glands, mouth) or laboratory-confirmed FMD virus
PlaceMae On, San Kamphaeng and San Sai districts, Chiang Mai Province
Time periodOutbreaks in 2016 (July–November); questionnaire survey February–June 2017
Comparison140 outbreak farms vs 307 non-outbreak farms

Task 2. Type of study and study design

Question: (1) What is the type of study? (2) Explain the study design: how farms were selected, when the outcome and exposures were measured, and the direction (backward or forward).

1. Type of study

An observational analytical study: a case-control study at farm level.

2. Study design

  • Selection: farms were chosen by outcome. Cases = dairy farms with an FMD outbreak in 2016 (from official outbreak records of the Department of Livestock Development, DLD). Controls = registered dairy farms in the same three districts without an outbreak.
  • Timing: the outcome had already happened (2016). Exposures were measured afterwards, by a face-to-face questionnaire (February–June 2017), and refer to the time before and during the outbreak.
  • Direction: backward (retrospective): from the outcome (outbreak or not) back to the exposures.
  • Measure of association: odds ratio (chi-square tests, then logistic regression).
Also accepted Because all registered farms were interviewed, some students describe it as a retrospective cross-sectional survey. This is acceptable if you explain that exposure and outcome were not followed forward in time and that the odds ratio is the measure.

Task 3. Dependent, independent and confounding variables

Question: Complete the table: dependent variable (name and how defined); independent variables (at least six); possible confounders and the reason they meet the three conditions in Section 2.2.
RoleModel answer
Dependent variablestatus: FMD outbreak on the farm (1 = outbreak farm, 0 = non-outbreak farm). Binary. Defined as at least one animal with typical clinical signs, or laboratory-confirmed FMD virus.
Independent variables (any six or more) PurchaseWithoutQ (bought a new cow without quarantine), selling (vehicle carrying stock entered the farm), marketVisit (visited cattle markets), NearRoad (near roadways), slaughter (within 5 km of a cattle abattoir), together (near shared grazing area), contact (neighbour farm within 500 m), previous (outbreak in previous year), staff_training (biosecurity training), coorstaffV (AI by more than one staff member). Others: faces, milk, water, waste, type, shared_equip; gender, age, expe, education, number of cattle.
Possible confounders (of PurchaseWithoutQ → outbreak) and reason staff_training. (1) Linked to the exposure: farms without trained staff more often bought cows without quarantine (60.6% vs 22.7% of farms in the dataset). (2) A risk factor for the outcome: training is linked to fewer outbreaks (OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.26–0.57). (3) Not on the causal path: training comes first and is not a result of buying cows without quarantine.

Number of cattle (herd size). Larger herds buy more animals (exposure) and have more contacts and visitors (outcome); herd size is not caused by the purchase practice.

District / farming area. Outbreak frequency and farm practices differed between districts; the paper kept district in the final model.

Farmer experience or education. Knowledge of biosecurity affects buying practice and disease risk.
Check each confounder against the three conditions (Section 2.2) (1) associated with the exposure; (2) a cause or predictor of the outcome, independent of the exposure; (3) not on the causal path between exposure and outcome. A factor on the causal path (for example selling, vehicles carrying stock, which may follow a purchase) is a mediator, not a confounder.

Task 4. Sampling method and units

Question: (1) Target population. (2) Sampling method for districts, cases and controls (Section 6.1). (3) Sampling unit and epidemiological unit: same or different? (4) Clustering: name the levels (Section 7.2).
ItemModel answer
1. Target populationDairy farms in the FMD-endemic area of northern Thailand (the results are meant to improve FMD prevention and control on dairy farms). The study population is the registered dairy farms in the three districts.
2. Sampling method Districts: non-probability, purposive selection. Three districts were chosen because they were close together and had many dairy farms.
Cases: complete enumeration (census) of outbreak farms, identified from official DLD outbreak records (140 farms).
Controls: all other registered dairy farms in the same districts (307 farms). There was no random selection; all farmers took part.
3. Sampling unit and epidemiological unitBoth are the dairy farm (the owner or manager answered the questionnaire). They are the same. A farm is a case if at least one animal had FMD. Cows are not analysed one by one.
4. ClusteringYes. Farms are nested in village → subdistrict → district (dataset: villagename, subdistrict, district). Farms in the same district share outbreak history, control measures and exposure to risk areas, so they are more alike than farms from different districts. The paper put district in the final model (AIC 524.89 with district vs 529.14 without). With many clusters, use a mixed-effects model or GEE.

Task 5. Sample size

Question: Kelsey case-control formula; p₂ from the controls in the dataset (PurchaseWithoutQ = 1); OR 2.0; 95% confidence; 80% power; 1 control per case; finite population N = 450; 15% dropout. Compare scenarios A, B, C with the real study (140 cases, 307 controls).

Step 1. Inputs

Exposure among controls: 91 of 285 control farms = 0.319 → p₂ = 0.32. OR = 2, α = 0.05 (Z = 1.96), power 80% (Z = 0.84), r = 1.

Step 2. Scenario A: basic calculation (1 control per case)

p₁ = OR × p₂ / [1 + p₂ (OR − 1)] = 2 × 0.32 / (1 + 0.32) = 0.4848
p̄ = (0.4848 + 0.32) / 2 = 0.4024, q̄ = 0.5976
n₁ = (Zα + Zβ)² × p̄ q̄ × (r + 1) / [r (p₁ − p₂)²]
= (1.96 + 0.84)² × 0.4024 × 0.5976 × 2 / (0.4848 − 0.32)² = 138.8 → 139 cases + 139 controls = 278

Step 3. Scenario B: apply the finite population correction (N = 450, on the total)

nf = n₀ / [1 + (n₀ − 1) / N] = 278 / (1 + 277/450) = 172.1 → 173
Round up to equal groups: 87 cases + 87 controls = 174

Step 4. Scenario C: add 15% dropout (after the finite population correction)

n = 174 / (1 − 0.15) = 204.7 → 205
Round up to equal groups: 103 cases + 103 controls = 206

Comparison with the real study and choice of scenario

  • The paper (140 cases, 307 controls = 447 farms) does not report a sample size calculation. All registered farms were interviewed (a census). The number of cases (140) is close to scenario A (139); the number of controls is about 2.2 per case.
  • The real study is larger than scenario C (206 farms) and so had more than enough power to detect OR = 2 at this exposure level.
  • Choice: scenario C. The population is small (450 farms), so the finite population correction is justified and saves about 100 farms. Dividing by 0.85 protects the study against farmers who cannot be reached or refuse. 103 cases are available (165 outbreak farms in the dataset), so it is feasible.
  • Dropout: divide by 0.85; do not multiply by 1.15.

Task 6. Appropriate analysis methods

Question: (1) Odds ratio or risk ratio for this design, and why. (2) Two methods to deal with confounding in the analysis. (3) What “adjusted” means and why the adjusted OR differs from the crude OR. (4) Analysis for a continuous variable such as number of cattle.
  1. Odds ratio. Farms were selected by outcome (cases and controls), so the investigator fixes the ratio of cases to controls and the risk of an outbreak cannot be estimated directly. The OR is valid for this design. Do not read it as a risk ratio: when the outcome is common (37% of farms in the dataset), the OR is further from 1 than the RR.
  2. Methods to deal with confounding in the analysis: (a) stratified analysis with the Mantel–Haenszel adjusted OR (one or a few confounders, Task 7B); (b) multivariable logistic regression (many confounders at once). (Restriction and matching are design-stage methods.)
  3. “Adjusted” means the association between the exposure and the outcome after the effect of the other factors in the analysis has been held constant (comparing farms that have the same values of the other factors). The adjusted OR differs from the crude OR because the crude OR mixes the effect of the exposure with the effect of the confounders. In the paper, the OR for buying cows without quarantine was 2.15 (crude) and 2.41 (adjusted). In the dataset, the crude OR 2.44 falls to 1.85 after adjustment for staff_training (Task 7B).
  4. Continuous variable (for example number of cattle): compare the mean between case and control farms with a t-test (Mann–Whitney or Wilcoxon test if skewed); or enter it into logistic regression as a continuous variable and report the OR per unit (for example per 10 cattle). Check that the relationship is roughly linear, or group it into categories (small, medium, large).
Steps used in the paper Univariable chi-square test, p ≤ 0.2 for screening → collinearity check (chi-square) → multivariable logistic regression with backward selection by AIC → Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p = 0.08, good fit) → ROC curve (AUC = 0.67, fair discrimination).

Paper: unadjusted OR 2.15 (95% CI 1.29–3.57, Table 2); adjusted OR 2.41 (95% CI 1.45–4.05, Table 3). Dataset: crude OR 2.44 (1.64–3.61).

Why they differ: (1) The dataset is simulated: different farms and a different number of cases and controls, so the estimates are similar but not the same. The crude OR (2.15) ignores them. Confounding can move the crude OR away from or toward 1, so the two numbers differ.

Step 3. Compare crude and MH odds ratios

Change = (crude OR − MH OR) / MH OR × 100 = (2.44 − 1.85) / 1.85 × 100 = 32%

The change is above the 10% rule of thumb, so staff_training is a confounder. It meets the conditions in Section 2.2: it is associated with the exposure (60.6% vs 22.7%), it is a risk factor for the outcome (crude OR 0.38) and it is not on the causal path. The crude OR was too high (positive confounding).

Step 4. Check homogeneity (can the strata be pooled?)

H₀: the OR is the same in both strata. p = 0.53 (> 0.05): no evidence that the OR differs between strata, so the strata ORs (1.65 and 2.16) can be pooled and the MH OR is a valid summary. If p were < 0.05, the effect would differ by stratum (effect modification) and you should report the stratum-specific ORs instead.

Step 5. Interpretation (2–3 sentences)

Model answer After adjusting for staff_training, the odds of an FMD outbreak were 1.85 times higher (95% CI 1.21–2.83; p = 0.004) on farms that bought a new cow without quarantine than on farms that did not. The crude OR (2.44) overstated the association because farms without trained staff were more likely to skip quarantine and also had more outbreaks. The OR is similar in both strata (p = 0.53), so one adjusted estimate is appropriate; the association is still significant after adjustment.

Step 6. Reflection

  • Why MH adjusts only one or a few factors: each extra confounder multiplies the number of strata (2 strata for one binary factor, 4 for two, 8 for three, …). The strata become small or empty, many have zero cells, and the estimate becomes unstable. Continuous confounders need grouping first.
  • For many factors at once: use multivariable logistic regression. In the dataset, a multivariable model including district, herd size, previous outbreak, staff_training and other factors gives an adjusted OR of about 2.6 for PurchaseWithoutQ, so adjustment for staff_training alone does not capture all confounding. (You are not asked to do this analysis.)

Task 8. Interpret the results and make recommendations

Question: (1) Write a sentence that interprets the OR for the exposures. (2) Explain why the exposure may increase or decrease the chance of an FMD outbreak. (3) Write practical recommendations for farmers and DLD to prevent and control FMD, each linked to a result.

1. Interpretation sentences

  • PurchaseWithoutQ (risk factor): “The odds of an FMD outbreak were 2.4 times higher on farms that bought a new cow without following quarantine than on farms that did not (OR 2.44; 95% CI 1.64–3.61; p < 0.001). The interval does not include 1, so the association is statistically significant.”
  • staff_training (protective factor): “The odds of an FMD outbreak were about 62% lower on farms where staff had biosecurity training (OR 0.38; 95% CI 0.26–0.57; p < 0.001).”
Say “odds”, not “risk” Farms were selected by outbreak status and the outbreak is common (37%), so the OR is larger than the RR would be. “2.4 times more likely” is not correct. An association is not proof of cause.

2. Why the exposure may increase or decrease the chance of an outbreak

Buying a cow without quarantine (↑)

  • FMD virus is highly contagious; one infected or incubating animal can introduce it into a susceptible herd.
  • A new cow can be incubating and can shed virus before clinical signs, so it may look healthy on arrival.
  • Some recovered cattle become carriers. Dealers mix cattle from different farms and markets without testing or quarantine (as described in the paper).
  • Without quarantine, the new cow immediately contacts resident cows, equipment and the milking routine.

Staff biosecurity training (↓)

  • Trained staff apply hygiene: clean boots and clothing, disinfect vehicles and equipment.
  • They recognise and report disease early and keep sick animals apart.
  • They are more likely to quarantine new cattle and to handle vaccines correctly (the paper links vaccination by non-official staff with higher risk).

Other examples: visiting cattle markets (OR 2.64) exposes farmers and clothing to infected animals; vehicles carrying stock (OR 1.62) and roadways (OR 1.81) carry virus between farms; a neighbour farm within 500 m (OR 1.80) allows spread by people, equipment and air.

Previous outbreak: the dataset and the paper differ In the dataset a previous outbreak is a risk factor (OR 3.43, 95% CI 2.28–5.15). In the paper it was protective (OR 0.44), explained by herd immunity and better biosecurity after an outbreak. The dataset is simulated. In real life either direction is possible, so each result needs a biological explanation.

Limitations to mention

Case-control data: exposure and outbreak timing cannot always be separated, and the OR is not the RR. Recall bias (the authors report 2–11 months of recall and no logbooks). Unmeasured confounding. Only registered farms were included. Many variables were tested, so some significant results may be chance. Farm-level results do not describe individual cows. The dataset is simulated.
